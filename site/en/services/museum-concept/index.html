Museum concept design

From “why does this museum exist” to an exhibition design ready to be built.

A museum does not begin with display cases or equipment. It begins with an answer to why it exists and what a person carries out of the door with them. Until that answer exists, every decision — about light, route, screen, vitrine — is made blind, and the argument about it can run forever.

A concept is the document that ends the argument. It states what the visitor should feel, how they move, what they do with their hands, where they stop and why. Everything else — layout, technology, budget — follows from it.

What the concept contains

  1. The core idea: why the museum is needed, for whom, and what it changes in a person.
  2. Visitor portraits: a schoolchild, a family, a specialist, a foreign guest — each with their own route and their own depth.
  3. Route scenario: the sequence of halls and meaning points, and the rhythm — where the tension is, where the pause, where the discovery.
  4. Exhibition inventory: every exhibit and interactive object described by what it does and why it stands exactly there.
  5. Spatial decisions: layouts, zoning, sketches of key objects, material and lighting references.
  6. The technical part: which technologies are needed, which are not, how it is serviced and what to do when it breaks.
  7. Phasing and budget ranges — so the concept can be defended, not only presented.

The five seconds and five minutes rule

Every object in the exhibition reads in five seconds and opens up over five minutes. In five seconds a child understands what to do — with no instructions on a label. In five minutes a specialist finds the depth they came for. One object works for every visitor at once, and we hold that constraint on each exhibit: if it can only be understood after reading the rules, the exhibit gets redesigned.

How the work goes

  1. Immersion. We read what already exists: the scholarly concept, the collection, research, earlier projects. We talk to curators and researchers — they hold material that is in no document.
  2. The meaning frame. We put into words what a person should feel. It is the shortest part of the document and the most expensive in consequences: everything else is derived from it.
  3. Scenario and route. We lay the meanings out across the halls and check the rhythm — and what happens to a person in the fortieth minute, when attention is already spent.
  4. Exhibition decisions. Every object gets a description: what the visitor does, what they see, what they take away.
  5. Defence. We assemble the document so it can be shown to a board, a ministry or an investor and produce a decision rather than another round of discussion.

What we work with

National museums, corporate and institutional museums, heritage sites, visitor centres at industrial facilities, science centres. A separate story is exhibitions about difficult subjects: technology, production, science, history that cannot be reduced to a slogan. The best way to explain something complex is to let people play with it, and we do that without losing accuracy: content is agreed with academic advisers rather than retold freely.

What drives cost and schedule

  • Floor area and the number of meaning zones — a concept for one hall and for a twenty-hall museum differ in kind, not in proportion.
  • The state of the source material: with a scholarly concept and a catalogued collection the work moves faster; without them, collecting comes first.
  • How many parties have to approve. One client and an interdepartmental board mean different schedules for the same amount of work.
  • Whether you need a concept for defence, or design documentation ready for construction.

Honestly about time: our museum projects have taken six months and more end to end. The concept is the first part of that. The conversation makes sense a year before opening, not three months before.

Frequently asked questions

How is a concept different from a design project?

A concept answers what and why; a design project answers how to build it. The concept defines what the visitor does and feels; the design project turns that into drawings, specifications and costings. A design project without a concept is a handsome room with nothing to do in it.

Can we order only the concept, without delivery?

Yes, and it is a common arrangement. The concept is a standalone document: you can defend it, seek funding with it and run a tender for delivery. We work both this way and full cycle through to opening.

Do you work with an existing museum?

Yes. Renewing an exhibition is a distinct kind of task: the building, the collection, the established route and a visitor who is used to it are all already there. The work is less about building something new and more about finding what stops the existing thing from working.

Who writes the scholarly content?

The scholarly content stays with the museum and its specialists — they know the material. Our part is turning that content into an experience: scenario, route, objects, interaction. Accuracy is verified by the client’s academic advisers.
