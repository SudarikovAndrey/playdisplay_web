Output equipment: how the exhibition speaks to a person

Light, image, sound, vibration — everything that reaches a person. The most visible part of the budget and the most treacherous: a four-metre screen people walk past without looking loses to a spyglass with a queue in front of it.

01
The principle hereThree numbers decide it: how much light is in the room, from what distance people look, and how many stand there at once. Not the diagonal, not the brand.
01.01Projection

Installation laser projector

The workhorse. Runs for years and never asks for a lamp

Laser instead of a lamp: 20,000 hours without a change, instant start, mounts upside down if it has to. Lamp models are no longer specified for new exhibitions — not because they throw less light, but because someone has to change the lamp, and there is nobody to do it.

How to size itThe number that matters is not lumens but lumens per square metre at your actual ambient light. The second is contrast: in a bright room the winner is not the brightest projector but the one whose black stays black.

Works when

  • An image larger than a physical screen — from three metres up to a whole wall
  • A surface that is not flat: a model, a vault, a column, a façade
  • The screen has to disappear when off: a black wall instead of a black rectangle

Falls short when

  • A room with windows and no blinds. Projection is the one technology sunlight kills outright, and no amount of lumens fixes that
  • Small type is needed: resolution divides across the area, and captions blur first
  • People walk between the viewer and the wall — there will be shadows

01.02Projection

Ultra-short-throw projector

You get the picture without the beam

Sits right against the surface and throws the image at almost a right angle. A person can walk up to the wall without blocking the frame with their own body — which is the whole point.

Works when

  • Interactive floors and tables: the visitor stands inside the image
  • Low ceilings, where a normal projector has nowhere to hang
  • People need to come right up to the surface and touch it

Falls short when

  • An uneven surface: the sharper the angle, the more every bump shows
  • Large sizes — beyond three or four metres the geometry starts misbehaving

01.03Projection

Rear projection

The projector hides behind the surface

Throws onto a translucent screen from behind. The viewer sees a glowing surface, never the machine or the beam, and casts no shadow — however many people crowd in.

Works when

  • A vitrine or window that should glow from within
  • Dense foot traffic right up against the image
  • The image has to sit together with a real object behind glass

Falls short when

  • No room behind: you need a service recess half a metre deep or more
  • People look from far to the side — translucent materials have a narrower viewing angle than an ordinary screen

01.04Projection

Mapping onto volume

Light lands along the facets of a real object

Not a device but a technique: the image is computed for the geometry of a model, a façade or a product. Needs an accurate model and blended projectors — in return the volume is real, not drawn.

Works when

  • There is a physical form — a site model, a building, a product, a landscape
  • The decision turns on scale: what stands where and how it connects
  • Several layers have to sit over one object — was, is, will be

Falls short when

  • The object is small: for anything under a metre a plain screen beside it is cheaper and sharper
  • The model is not built yet — calibration is done against the real thing, not the drawing

01.05LED screens

Flat modular screen

A wall of any size that sunlight cannot bother

A module is a tile with its own LEDs, power and board. They build into a canvas of any shape, and a broken tile is replaced one tile at a time. One question is settled before purchase: is there half a metre behind for servicing, or must the modules come out from the front.

How to size itPixel pitch is the distance between LEDs in millimetres. A rough rule: comfortable viewing distance in metres roughly equals the pitch in millimetres. A 2.5 mm pitch means two and a half metres and further. Check on a sample in your own room rather than from the vendor's table: brightness and moiré only show in the flesh.

Works when

  • A bright room, an atrium, a window facing the street — sunlight is no obstacle
  • Non-standard proportions: a long ribbon, a vertical, a square across a wall
  • Round-the-clock operation: an LED does not fade the way a projector lamp does
  • The screen is an architectural element in its own right

Falls short when

  • People come right up to it: at a 2 mm pitch the picture breaks into dots at about two metres, and «just take a finer pitch» is a different budget entirely
  • Deep black is needed in a dark room: an LED glows a little even when off
  • Weight and power: a 3×2 m canvas is already a question for the wall's load rating
  • Filming: without frequency sync the video will show banding

01.06LED screens

Flexible modules

A screen that bends to fit

The same LEDs on a rubber backing: the canvas wraps a column, lies over a vault, follows the wave of a wall. Where a flat panel would leave a seam, this gives one continuous surface.

Works when

  • A column, an arch, a vault, a curved wall — anywhere a flat canvas would leave a seam
  • Shape matters more than resolution: from a distance the silhouette reads, not the pixel
  • Temporary builds: a flexible canvas is lighter and forgives an imprecise frame

Falls short when

  • Fine pitch: the more flexible the backing, the coarser the available pitch
  • The radius is limited — get the minimum figure before designing the frame
  • Shorter service life than flat: bending works against the solder, and it does not enjoy being rehung again and again

Manufacturers

01.07LED screens

Volumetric forms: sphere, cylinder, cube

This screen has no viewer's seat

Ready-made LED spheres, cylinders, cubes and rings. The image wraps the whole form, so the object works for people circling it and reads the same from a balcony and from below.

Watch out in the specContent for such a form is computed differently: flat video does not stretch onto a sphere without distortion. It needs an unwrap made for that exact geometry, and that is the work budgets routinely forget to include.

Works when

  • An atrium, a lobby, the centre of a room — a point people walk around
  • A globe, a planet, a core, a molecule — when the subject itself is round
  • An object that must read equally from a balcony and from below

Falls short when

  • Text and diagrams: nothing is legible across curvature
  • A tight budget: a non-standard form always costs more than the same area of flat
  • Low ceilings — the object has to hang with clearance over people's heads

01.08LED screens

Kinetic LED screen

A canvas that moves through the air

Modules hang on winches and change position through the show: the screen parts, gathers, becomes a wave. Image and movement are one score — you cannot reproduce it with a video.

How to size itTreated as lifting equipment: load, secondary safeties, exclusion zone underneath. This is a matter of permits and inspection regime, not of design.

Works when

  • An opening, a launch, a show — an event with a beginning and a finale
  • An atrium with a high ceiling where the movement reads from below
  • An effect that cannot be reproduced by playing a video

Falls short when

  • A permanent exhibition: winches need inspection regimes and permits, like any lifting gear
  • People underneath the rig — that is a safety calculation, not a preference
  • A quiet room: the drives are audible

01.09Panels

Professional panel and video wall

Dull, dependable, legible

The industrial relative of a television: round-the-clock operation, portrait mounting, remote management, years without being switched off. A consumer TV in an exhibition lasts until its first summer.

Works when

  • Text, drawings and archive documents have to be read
  • The screen hangs for a long time and simply has to work
  • A modest budget: the best ratio of cost to legibility

Falls short when

  • The job is impression, not reading: a panel does not create an event
  • A video wall of several panels: the bezels are always visible, and across a single image they read as a grid

01.10Panels

Transparent screen

Data over the real object

The image hangs on glass and the exhibit shows through. The original stays original while cutaways, labels and animation lie over it as a layer. Switch it off and only glass remains.

Works when

  • A vitrine holding an original that needs explaining without being covered
  • A cutaway, a diagram or a trajectory laid over a real product
  • When it is switched off, only glass should remain

Falls short when

  • A bright room: a transparent screen is the first to lose to ambient light
  • A busy background behind the glass — the image loses legibility
  • A dense, saturated picture is needed: transparency works against contrast

01.11Volume and illusion

Pseudo-hologram: Pepper's ghost

A reflection in angled glass. Two hundred years and counting

The picture from a screen reflects in a semi-transparent film and appears to hang in mid-air. Nineteenth-century theatre knew this trick, and it is still the most dependable of the «holograms» — there is nothing in it to break.

Works when

  • A small object with one or two people gathered around it
  • A dark zone: the illusion is built on glowing in darkness
  • A floating effect without glasses and without teaching the visitor anything

Falls short when

  • A delegation of twenty: the illusion holds only within a narrow angle
  • A bright room — the effect disappears entirely
  • Small captions: put them on a separate screen alongside

Manufacturers

  • Realfictionготовые витрины-кубы
  • Musionсценические установки большого размера
01.12Volume and illusion

Holographic fan display

LEDs on spinning blades. Cheap and impossible to ignore

A strip of LEDs turns fast enough that the eye assembles the trail into a whole image hanging in the air. Installed in an hour, gangs into a wall — that is how the big «floating» objects are made.

Works when

  • A window, an entrance, a point that has to catch attention
  • A short bright image — a logo, an object, a sign
  • A wow effect on a small budget
  • Retail and events, where the unit moves from venue to venue

Falls short when

  • A museum exhibition proper: a spinning part at hand height is a safety and noise question
  • A long story: neither the resolution nor the brightness carries content
  • Filming on a phone — the camera catches the strobing
  • Bright daylight washes the illusion out

01.13Volume and illusion

Light-field display

Volume without glasses. The honest kind

The screen emits dozens of viewpoints at once: the eyes see different things and the object turns as you move your head. Sizes are still modest and the price matches — but this is real volume, not an illusion.

Works when

  • A single object to be examined from every side — a product, a molecule, a part
  • Volume with no glasses and no instructions
  • An exhibit for one or two people at a time

Falls short when

  • A large audience and large sizes
  • A limited budget: for the same money you get an interactive 3D model on an ordinary screen, and people understand it just as well

01.14Volume and illusion

Autostereoscopic screen

Volume without glasses at panel money

An optical raster on an ordinary panel gives each eye its own pixels. Cheaper and larger than light-field; you pay in the narrow zone where the effect holds.

Works when

  • A vitrine or pylon that people approach one at a time
  • A product to be shown in volume without handing out glasses
  • Volume is wanted at the price of a panel, not of an installation

Falls short when

  • A group: the viewing zone is narrow, one step aside and the volume falls apart
  • Horizontal resolution is divided between viewpoints: small type does not survive
  • Long viewing tires the eyes more than an ordinary screen

01.15Headsets and windows

Virtual reality

The strongest impression and the longest queue

The headset closes off the world: a person ends up where they physically cannot be. One person at a time experiences it — and the whole schedule is counted from that number.

How to size itWork it out before purchase: a 4-minute session plus a minute to change over = 12 people an hour per headset. A group of 30 is two and a half hours on one unit.

Works when

  • A place you cannot go: underground, in orbit, inside a ruined building, inside a body
  • A separate zone with a queue and an attendant
  • A session that can be sold on its own

Falls short when

  • The main route of the exhibition: the queue forms and everything stops
  • No staff nearby: fitting, adjusting, disinfecting, removing
  • A school group on a timetable — the arithmetic does not work

01.16Headsets and windows

Augmented reality on a device

A layer over the real room

A tablet or phone shows the camera feed with what the room does not contain laid over it: a building completed, a mechanism running, a character. Cheap in hardware, expensive in content.

Works when

  • Completing what has not survived: a ruin, a lost interior, a decommissioned machine

Falls short when

  • Relying on the visitor's phone with no guidance: only a fraction ever get it running

01.17Sound and body

Sound shower, directional audio

Audible here, silent one step away

Sound confined to a narrow zone: the visitor at the exhibit hears the story, a neighbour a metre away hears nothing, and nobody needs headphones. It cures the chief affliction of multimedia exhibitions — ten exhibits talking at once and none of them audible.

Two principlesTwo different principles, and confusing them is expensive. A parabolic dome is an ordinary loudspeaker in a reflector: the sound is honest and full, the zone under the dome is about a metre across, and there is no distortion. An ultrasonic parametric emitter is a narrow beam of ultrasound that turns into sound in the air itself: the zone is far narrower and reaches further, but the bass is lost and speech sounds drier. For voice and atmosphere, the dome; for a pinpoint beam across a room, the parametric.

Works when

  • Several exhibits with sound in one room
  • A story that only the person standing here needs
  • Headphones are unwelcome — foot traffic or hygiene

Falls short when

  • Full bass and enveloping sound are needed
  • High ceilings and heavy background noise: the zone smears
  • Visitors approach from several directions

01.18Sound and body

Installation audio and spatial sound

Sound that has a place in the room

Sources placed around the room give sound direction and distance: a footstep on the left, a rumble overhead, a voice from one point. Half the sense of presence is made here, not on the screen.

Works when

  • A large room with a single score — a panorama, a reconstruction, an immersion

  • There is a score with rhythm: silence, build, pause

  • Scattered stands each with their own content — it will be a mess
  • A reverberant room with no acoustic treatment: fix the ceiling before buying speakers

Headphones and personal audio

When the story is long and personal

From a classic audio guide to streaming audio to the visitor's phone. The only way to deliver a long text, several languages and different depths from one and the same exhibit.

01.20Sound and body

Vibration, wind, heat, scent

What is remembered longer than the picture

Shakers in floors and benches, fans, heat, scent. Pennies next to screens, and it works disproportionately hard: the body remembers before the head does.

Works when

  • A moment that should be remembered by the body: a blast, a launch, a storm, a descent
  • Recreating an environment — the heat of a workshop, the damp of a trench, wind on a deck
  • A young audience: a physical impression lands before text does

Falls short when

  • A constant background effect — people habituate within a minute and stop noticing
  • Scent: it stays in the room longer than intended, and not everyone likes it
  • Allergies and sensitivities — there must be a way to bypass the zone

01.21Sound and body

Controlled light

The cheapest way to change a scene

Light in an exhibition does not illuminate, it narrates: singles out, extinguishes, leads, sets the rhythm. It runs on the same cues as everything else at a fraction of any screen.

Works when

  • The route needs rhythm: darkness, flash, stillness

Falls short when

  • Scripted light fights the room's general lighting: separate the two from the start
  • Conservation requirements — lux levels and UV are agreed with the curator
  • Flashing and strobes: a medical constraint, not an aesthetic one

01.22Motion and matter

Kinetics and mechanics

The exhibit genuinely moves

Actuators and mechanisms: a model opens out, a machine rotates, a cutaway parts. A different order of impression — the object is real, and it reads from the far end of the hall.

How to size itThe one group in this whole library that needs a safety risk assessment: a moving part at a child's hand height is not a design question but an injury question.

Works when

  • How a product is made: shown in section, with the real form opening up
  • A room where there are already too many screens and they have stopped working
  • An event visible from the far end of the hall

Falls short when

  • Mechanics within reach of visitors: count the duty cycle and the pinch protection
  • A quiet room — drives are audible
  • Someone has to maintain it: moving things wear out, screens do not

01.23Motion and matter

Electronic ink

A label you can rewrite

The display draws power only while the image changes and reads like paper — at any angle, with nothing glowing into anyone's face. It cures an old museum ache: new attribution and a new language on the caption.

Works when

  • No power at the point: batteries last months

Falls short when

  • Colour: colour models are noticeably paler and dearer

01.24Motion and matter

Drone show

A screen the size of the sky

A swarm holds formation to a computed programme and draws three-dimensional figures in the air. The only way to get a picture hundreds of metres across with no structure at all — in many places it has already replaced fireworks: quieter, cleaner, repeatable.

Watch out in the specIf someone's presentation still says «Intel Shooting Star», the document is out of date. Intel left this business in 2022, selling the division along with its fleet; shows today are flown by other companies on their own platforms.
How to size itThe resolution of the picture equals the number of drones. A hundred drones gives the outline of a recognisable symbol; a thousand gives a moving narrative. Budget follows the number of airframes and flying days, not the length of the show.

Works when

  • An opening, an anniversary, a city celebration — an event under open sky
  • A scale that cannot be built: hundreds of metres in height and width
  • A place where fireworks are barred by noise, fire risk or animal welfare

Falls short when

  • Wind and precipitation: the weather window is narrow, and a reserve date belongs in the contract rather than in conversation
  • Approvals: airspace, zone, time — that is weeks, not days
  • Indoors: a hall flies a handful of drones, not hundreds, and the effect is quite different
  • Fine graphics and text: resolution is the number of drones, and there are hundreds, not millions
