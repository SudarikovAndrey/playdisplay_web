Processing and control: what the visitor never sees and what holds it all up

Between what a person did and what they saw lies a layer that appears in no photograph of an exhibition: machines, engines, network and room control. Specifications cut this part first, because it cannot be shown to a board. A year later it is precisely this part that decides whether the exhibition runs or stands idle.

03
The principle hereA simple acceptance rule: an exhibit must come up on one button pressed by an attendant, and the content must be changeable by museum staff. If both answers are no, you have not bought a system — you have bought a dependency on a contractor.

Machines

The hardware everything is computed on. The choice is driven not by power but by who will restart it and how.

03.01

Media player appliance

A box that simply plays

A small device with no moving parts and no operating system in the usual sense: switch it on and it runs the schedule. It does not hang, does not update at the wrong moment, and does not show a desktop in the middle of a gallery.

Works when

  • A film or a schedule with no interactivity
  • Many identical points: ten screens with the same content
  • Nobody to service it — you need a thing that simply does not break

Falls short when

  • Genuine interactivity and real-time 3D — beyond it
  • Content that depends on external data and logic

03.02

Industrial computer

No fan, no dust, no surprises

A computer in a sealed case with passive cooling, rated for round-the-clock work and for years of the same model staying available. That last point matters more than speed: in three years you will need exactly the same one as a replacement.

Watch out in the specAn ordinary office build inside a plinth survives until its first summer: dust settles on the fan, the temperature climbs, and the machine throttles out on the busiest day. This is the commonest mistake in ready-made specifications.

Works when

  • An exhibit inside a sealed box or in a dusty room
  • Running for months without being switched off
  • A guarantee that the same unit can be bought in three years

Falls short when

  • Heavy real-time graphics: that needs a discrete card and active cooling
  • A one-week trade show — overpaying

03.03

Single-board computer

Thirty dollars instead of three hundred

A computer the size of a palm, with no moving parts and almost no power draw. It does not replace a workstation, but it covers half the points in an exhibition: a sensor, a button, sound on a trigger, lighting, simple video, polling equipment. Everywhere a full PC would otherwise stand «just in case».

Works when

  • Many small points: a sensor, a button, a lamp, an audio response
  • The exhibit hides in a plinth with no room and no ventilation
  • Spares matter: keeping three on a shelf is cheap
  • A link to the physical world — sensors and actuators connect directly

Falls short when

  • Real-time 3D and heavy video: not for this
  • The memory card is the weak point; with frequent hard power-offs it dies first — cured by booting from an SSD and a read-only filesystem
  • Supply in batches: in a shortage, price and lead time triple
How to size itThe main question is not performance but how it survives being switched off at the mains at closing time. A board configured to read from a protected partition lasts years; a stock install on a memory card lasts months.

03.04

Graphics workstation

When the picture is computed right now

A machine with a professional graphics card and several outputs. Needed where the image is not played back but born: real-time 3D, reaction to a person, several synchronised screens driven from one scene.

Works when

  • An interactive 3D scene reacting to visitors
  • Several projectors from one source, blended into a single field
  • Content that depends on data and changes on the fly

Falls short when

  • It plays a film — several times the money for nothing
  • No ventilation in the plinth: this machine runs genuinely hot

03.05

Neural networks on site

Computes here, sends nothing out

A compact module that runs recognition models right there in the room, without internet and without a cloud. Frames never leave the museum — which removes half the legal questions before they arise.

Works when

  • Recognition where data may not be sent outside
  • Unstable internet, or none at all
  • Latency is critical: the response must be immediate

Falls short when

  • The heaviest models: on-site capacity is always less than cloud capacity
  • A model that needs frequent updates — on site that is manual work

03.06

The operating system of the room

A dull choice that echoes for two years

Windows, macOS or Linux — decided once, and it determines who will be able to service the exhibition. Chosen not by taste but by what the required software runs on and who will fix it at three o'clock on a Saturday afternoon.

Two principlesWindows runs nearly everything: media servers, TouchDesigner, Unity and Unreal builds, drivers for any hardware. The price is updates that arrive at the wrong moment and reboot the gallery; cured by a long-term-servicing edition, not by a «defer» checkbox. macOS is reliable and quiet, good for audio and video, but the software choice is narrower and it is harder to put a machine in a plinth and leave it for five years. Linux is free, does not reboot itself and is ideal for players and single-board machines; professional media servers for it are rare.

Works when

  • Windows: interactivity, 3D, unusual hardware, media servers
  • macOS: audio, video, colour accuracy, a small quiet point
  • Linux: schedules, players, single-board machines, anything that must simply not fall over

Falls short when

  • A mixed estate of three systems in one gallery: there will be nobody to service it
  • Choosing by the contractor's habit rather than by the software — the commonest reason a museum ends up tied to one supplier
Our takeWhatever is chosen, disable automatic updates and automatic sleep, set the scenario to launch on boot, and enable automatic login. Test it at acceptance the simple way: pull the power and see whether the exhibit comes back up on its own, with no keyboard.

Media servers

A class of its own: machine and software together, whose job is to bring many images and many outputs into one governable picture.

03.07

Media server

One brain for a whole projection wall

It feeds a dozen outputs, blends projectors into a single field, corrects geometry for a curved surface and holds all of it to a timeline. Where there are more than two projectors, without one you assemble a zoo of machines that nobody will be able to service.

Works when

  • Several projectors into one field: a panorama, a dome, a vault
  • Mapping onto volume with exact geometry
  • The show runs to a schedule and must start itself

Falls short when

  • One screen with a film — an order of magnitude more than needed
  • Deep interactivity: a media server is strong at playback, not at logic

03.08

Warping and geometry correction

So the seam cannot be seen

Software that works out from camera shots how to distort each image so that several projectors merge into one field with no seam and no curvature on an uneven wall. Recomputed in minutes rather than overnight.

Works when

  • More than one projector on one surface
  • A surface that is not flat: a vault, a dome, a corner, a model
  • Calibration will have to be repeated — buildings move and equipment shifts

Falls short when

  • One projector on a flat wall — nothing is needed
  • No way to place a camera where the viewer stands

Engines and environments

What the content itself is made in. The choice here decides not the picture but who will be able to change it later.

03.09

Unity

The workhorse of interactive exhibits

A game engine that has become the standard for museum interactivity: real-time 3D, work with any sensor, builds for anything from a gallery screen to a VR headset to a visitor's phone. There are more specialists on the market for it than for any other tool on this list.

Works when

  • An interactive exhibit with real logic and state
  • One body of content across several devices: gallery, headset, phone
  • You want a contractor who can easily be replaced later

Falls short when

  • A simple film — overkill
  • Top-grade photorealistic architecture: Unreal is stronger there

Manufacturers

  • Unityдвижок и среда разработки

03.10

Unreal Engine

When the credibility of the image decides it

An engine with the strongest lighting and materials out of the box. Chosen where the image has to be indistinguishable from a photograph: architecture, interiors, landscape, a product at advertising quality.

Works when

  • Architecture and territory that must look real
  • A large world with distant views
  • Material matters in itself: metal, fabric, glass

Falls short when

  • Modest hardware: the machine requirements are noticeably higher
  • Simple logic on a small screen — heavy for the job

  • Unreal Engineдвижок, бесплатен для невыпускаемых проектов
03.11

TouchDesigner

An environment for things that never repeat

A node-based environment for generative graphics, signal work and live reaction to data. Where Unity builds a scene, TouchDesigner builds a flow: an image born out of movement, sound and sensors that never looks the same twice.

Works when

  • Generative content: an image born from data or movement
  • Many inputs and outputs at once: cameras, sensors, projectors, sound
  • Fast rework on site, during the install

Falls short when

  • Classic game logic with levels and states
  • A wide choice of replacement contractors: there are markedly fewer specialists

Manufacturers

  • Derivative TouchDesignerсреда, бесплатная лицензия для обучения
03.12

Notch and Resolume

Tools for live performance

Notch makes effects computed in real time and runs inside media servers. Resolume is live playback and mapping from a console. Both are about performance and event more than about a permanent exhibition.

Works when

  • An event, an opening, a show on a façade
  • The effect has to react to a live signal — sound, camera, operator
  • You need a desk that a person sits at and drives the show by hand

Falls short when

  • A permanent exhibition with no operator
  • Complex branching interaction logic

Manufacturers

  • Notchэффекты в реальном времени, работает внутри медиасерверов
  • Resolumeживой показ и маппинг
03.13

Creative coding

Tools where graphics are written, not assembled

Open environments in which the image is born from code and data: sound visualisation, generative patterns, reaction to the flow of visitors. Free, needing no licences, and happy on modest hardware — but a contractor for them is harder to find than for Unity.

Works when

  • Generative content: a pattern out of data, sound, movement
  • A small budget with no software licences
  • A prototype that has to be tested in a day before a decision is made
  • A long life: open source is not withdrawn or moved to a subscription

Falls short when

  • A complex scene with 3D and physics — faster in a game engine
  • Handing the project to another contractor: markedly fewer specialists
  • Vendor support is wanted — by definition it does not exist

Signal and operations

The part cut from the budget first and remembered in year two. Everything here pays for itself with a single call-out that never had to happen.

03.14

Signal distribution

Distance decides more than you would think

An ordinary video cable works to about fifteen metres; beyond that you need twisted pair with dedicated transmitters, fibre, or transport over the network. The decision is taken while the walls are still open — afterwards it is expensive.

Works when

  • Machine in the rack room, screen in the gallery: separating them is right
  • Many sources and many screens with switching
  • A historic building where chasing walls is not allowed — the network saves you

Falls short when

  • Network transport adds latency, and interactivity does not forgive it
  • Cheap extenders produce dropouts that take months to track down

Manufacturers

  • Extronинсталляционный стандарт коммутации
  • Kramerширокая линейка, доступен в РФ
  • NDIизображение по обычной сети
03.15

Room control

One button for the whole exhibition

The system that switches everything on to a schedule in the morning, shuts it down at night, and gives the attendant one panel with sensible buttons instead of ten remotes. It is what decides whether the museum can open without an engineer.

Works when

  • More than five devices in a room
  • Opening and closing to a schedule, with nobody present
  • An attendant has to be able to restart an exhibit unaided

Falls short when

  • One or two exhibits: a socket with a timer does the same
  • No trained person at all: this too needs a minimum of competence

03.16

Monitoring and remote access

Learn about the fault before the visitor does

The system reports that an exhibit has gone dark, that a lamp has reached end of life or that a machine rebooted overnight. Without it you hear about the fault from an online review rather than from the equipment.

Works when

  • An exhibition of more than one room
  • The contractor is not in your city
  • Equipment under warranty, where running hours have to be evidenced

Falls short when

  • Security will not permit external access — settle that beforehand, not after install

03.17

Content management system

So text can change without the contractor

The panel through which the museum edits captions itself, changes photographs, adds a language and updates the schedule. The most important line in the acceptance list and the most frequently absent.

Works when

  • The content will change — and it will
  • Several languages, added over time
  • Temporary exhibitions inside a permanent display

Falls short when

  • A one-off exhibit that will never change — rare, but it happens
  • Nobody to use it: a panel without a trained member of staff is dead
Our takeDemand it explicitly at acceptance: have a member of museum staff change a caption on an exhibit, in front of you, without phoning the contractor. If that is impossible, you have bought a dependency.
03.18

Machine synchronisation

So adjacent screens stay in step

When an image is spread across several machines, each runs on its own clock — and within a minute of playback the drift is visible to the eye. Synchronisation binds them to a common time: frame to frame, sound to image.

Works when

  • A panorama or wall assembled from several machines
  • Sound and image from different sources that have to coincide
  • The show loops all day and must not drift apart

Falls short when

  • One machine for everything — nothing is needed
  • Screens far apart and never seen at the same time

Manufacturers

  • NVIDIA Quadro Syncаппаратная синхронизация выходов
  • PTP / IEEE 1588единое время по сети
03.19

Backup and recovery

A plan for the day it does break

An image of every machine, a copy of the content and a written recovery procedure. Not equipment but a clause in the contract — yet without it, replacing a burnt-out computer turns from an hour into a week.

Works when

  • An exhibition that has to open tomorrow whatever happens
  • The contractor may leave the market — the museum will not
  • Uniform equipment: one image covers ten machines

Falls short when

  • Not a single person who will follow the procedure — then you need a service contract
Our takeAsk for these separately at acceptance: machine images, content source files and a printed recovery procedure. This is what the museum owns outright, rather than borrows for the duration.

What we do in all this

We start from the effect we want, not from a particular technology. First we settle what the visitor should walk out with, and only then what to build it from. We are tied to no manufacturer, so the solution gets picked for the job rather than for what is familiar or already sitting in a warehouse. Hence a library written as a catalogue of limits — so you can see where a technology is genuinely strong and where it gets specified out of habit.

Supply is handled by our partners: any equipment in this library, on the best terms for the client. Our part is the story, the content, the interaction, the software and the integration. We build complex hardware-and-software systems and answer for the whole thing working as one, rather than as a pile of boxes from different vendors.
